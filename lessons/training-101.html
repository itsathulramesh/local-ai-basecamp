Training 101: LoRA, QLoRA and what fits your GPU

This lesson turns the gradient-descent idea from lesson 7.1 into the practical facts of fine-tuning on a home NVIDIA GPU: why full training is out of reach, how LoRA makes it possible, and exactly how much VRAM you need.

What you will learn

  • Explain why full fine-tuning needs far more memory than inference
  • Explain LoRA with the matrix picture, and count its parameters
  • Explain QLoRA and when not to use it
  • Size a training run for your own card

Why full fine-tuning does not fit

Training every weight of a model with the Adam optimizer needs, per weight: the weight itself, its gradient, and two running averages for Adam, plus a full-precision master copy in many setups. That is roughly 16 bytes per parameter. For a 9B model: about 150 GB, before counting the activations of your training text. No home GPU comes close.

LoRA: train a small patch

Concept: LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation)

Freeze every original weight. Next to each chosen matrix W, add two small trainable matrices, A and B. The layer now computes W·x + (alpha/r) · B·(A·x). Only A and B are trained. Together they are the adapter: a file of tens of megabytes that you can keep, swap, stack or merge into W later.

Think of the base model as a signed program you never modify, and the adapter as a patch file.

The matrix picture

  W (frozen): 4096 x 4096 = 16,777,216 numbers

  A: 16 x 4096   =  65,536 trainable
  B: 4096 x 16   =  65,536 trainable
  LoRA total     = 131,072  (0.78% of W)

  x (4096) --A--> 16 numbers --B--> 4096 numbers    (bottleneck width r = 16)
  • Rank r is the width of that bottleneck: how many independent "directions of change" each adapter can learn. Typical values are 8 to 64.
  • Alpha scales the adapter's effect by alpha / r. Raising alpha acts like raising the learning rate for the adapter.
  • B starts at zero, so at step 0 the adapter changes nothing and the model behaves exactly like the base.

Across a whole 9B model, a rank-16 adapter on the MLP layers is about 25 million trainable parameters, around a quarter of one percent. The optimizer state for that is under half a gigabyte instead of 150 GB. That is the entire reason individuals can fine-tune at all.

Which matrices get adapters

The setting is called target modules. Current practice is to target both attention and MLP projections (q_proj, k_proj, v_proj, o_proj, gate_proj, up_proj, down_proj), which tends to beat attention-only LoRA. Hybrid models add their own names, such as out_proj for Qwen3.5's linear-attention layers. You met these names in lesson 7.2.

QLoRA: shrink the frozen part too

Concept: QLoRA

LoRA on top of a base model held in 4-bit. The frozen weights take about a quarter of the memory. They are expanded on the fly for each calculation, while the small adapter stays in 16-bit and receives all the updates.

QLoRA is the standard way to fit 7B to 8B models of many families (Llama, Gemma, Mistral) onto consumer cards. One important exception: Unsloth's guide for the Qwen3.5 family says it does not recommend 4-bit QLoRA training for those models because of larger-than-normal quantization differences, and recommends 16-bit LoRA instead. Always check the current guide for the model family you train.

What fits your card

Unsloth's published figures for 16-bit LoRA on the Qwen3.5 family (approximate, at modest sequence length and batch size 1 to 2):

Training is stricter than running

Running a model can spill into system RAM (lesson 3.3). Training cannot do that usefully: the weights, adapter, gradients and activations all need to be in VRAM. So a 4 GB card that happily runs a 12B model can only train about 1B-class models.

On a 4 GB card (GTX 1650 and similar)
  • Train locally: models under about 1B, such as Qwen3.5-0.8B or Qwen3-0.6B, with short sequences (512 to 1,024 tokens) and batch size 1. Perfect for learning the whole pipeline.
  • Train 2B to 4B for free on Google Colab's 16 GB T4 GPU with Unsloth's notebooks, then download the GGUF and run it on your PC. A 4B fine-tune runs fully on your GPU; even a 9B one runs with offload.
  • No bf16: GTX 16-series and RTX 20-series cards (Turing) have no native bfloat16 support, so training uses float16 instead. Unsloth detects this automatically. The free Colab T4 is the same generation, which is why Unsloth's free notebooks work this way too.
Memory levers when you run out
  • Shorter max_seq_length (activations grow with it).
  • Batch size 1 with more gradient accumulation steps.
  • Gradient checkpointing (recompute instead of store). Unsloth enables its own version.
  • An 8-bit optimizer (adamw_8bit).
  • A smaller model. A well-trained 2B often beats a badly-fitted 9B.

Catastrophic forgetting

Training hard on a narrow task can erode general abilities: the model gets great at your JSON format and worse at everything else. LoRA forgets less than full fine-tuning, but it still happens. Protect against it with few epochs, a modest learning rate, some general examples mixed into your data, and a "regression set" of unrelated questions you check after training (lesson 9.5).

Check yourself

Why does LoRA make fine-tuning possible on a home GPU?

  1. It removes the need for data
  2. Only a tiny add-on is trained, so gradients and optimizer state are tiny
  3. It runs on the CPU

Optimizer memory scales with trainable parameters, and LoRA cuts those to well under 1%.

You want to fine-tune Qwen3.5-4B on a 12 GB card. Which setup matches Unsloth's guidance?

  1. 4-bit QLoRA, to save memory
  2. Full fine-tuning
  3. 16-bit LoRA with short sequences and batch size 1

Unsloth advises against QLoRA for Qwen3.5. About 10 GB is needed, so keep activations small.

At training step 0, how does a LoRA model behave?

  1. Exactly like the base model, because B starts at zero
  2. Randomly
  3. It refuses everything

B·A is zero until training grows it.
