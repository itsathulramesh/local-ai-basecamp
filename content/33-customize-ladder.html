"Customize my model" can mean six very different things, from a one-line system prompt to editing weights. Each rung is more powerful, more expensive and harder to undo than the one below. This lesson helps you pick the lowest rung that does the job.

What you will learn

  • Name the six ways to change a model's behaviour
  • Match a goal to the cheapest method that achieves it
  • Know what fine-tuning is good and bad at before you spend hours on it

Why fine-tuning facts backfires

A few hundred examples mentioning a fact do not reliably store it. The model learns that it should sound confident about that kind of detail, then invents details it never saw. Research has found that fine-tuning on knowledge the model did not already have tends to increase hallucination. Keep facts in retrieval, where you can inspect and update them, and use fine-tuning to teach the model how to work with them.

A decision checklist

  1. Have I written a clear system prompt with two or three examples, and tested it on ten real inputs?
  2. Is the missing piece knowledge? If yes, build RAG.
  3. Have I tried a newer or larger model that still fits my GPU?
  4. Is it one behavioural axis (length, tone)? Try steering.
  5. Do I have, or can I make, a few hundred high-quality examples of exactly the behaviour I want? If yes, fine-tune.

Check yourself

You want a model that answers questions about your 300-page product manual. What is the right tool?

  1. Fine-tune on the manual
  2. A longer system prompt
  3. RAG over the manual

Facts belong in retrieval. Fine-tuning teaches habits, not reliable knowledge.

Your model ignores a format instruction one time in five, even with examples. What next?

  1. Fine-tune on a few hundred correct examples of that format
  2. Pre-train a new model
  3. Raise the temperature

Consistent formats are a classic fine-tuning win once prompting plateaus.
